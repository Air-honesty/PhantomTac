PhantomTac: Hijacking Robotic Tactile Systems with Deploy-and-forget Tags

This page provides the anonymized artifact package, raw datasets, source code, and demo materials for evaluating PhantomTac, a passive physical-layer attack against magnetic tactile sensing systems.

Abstract

Tactile perception is crucial for precise robotic manipulation and robotic arm grasping, providing an important foundation for dexterous closed-loop force control. In this study, we investigate a physical-layer loophole, called PhantomTac, in magnetic tactile sensors. By altering the local magnetic field around the sensor, an attacker can spoof the force interpreted by the tactile system.

PhantomTac can be deployed by attaching a thin, passive, battery-free magnetic tag to the surface of an object. The tag can be concealed by ordinary labels, such as barcode stickers, enabling a stealthy deploy-and-forget attack model.

When a robotic system interacts with a tagged object, spoofed tactile feedback can trigger unsafe control decisions. Normal-force spoofing may cause over-force grasping and object damage, whereas shear-force spoofing may lead to unstable grasping and object dropping. End-to-end evaluations show high attack effectiveness under practical robotic manipulation scenarios.

Magnetic tactile sensors

Magnetic tactile sensors are an emerging tactile sensing modality for robotic manipulation. They usually embed small magnets inside a soft elastomer and use magnetic field sensors to infer deformation and contact forces.

Sensing principle

When external contact deforms the soft structure, the relative position between the embedded magnet and the magnetic field sensor changes. The measured magnetic field variation is then mapped to tactile force information.

Robotic applications

These sensors are useful for robotic grasping, dexterous manipulation, slip detection, contact localization, and closed-loop force control because they can provide compact and multi-dimensional tactile feedback.

Security implication

Because the sensing signal is derived from magnetic field measurements, external magnetic sources can become a physical-layer attack surface. PhantomTac studies how passive magnetic tags can exploit this property.

Attack overview

PhantomTac is organized around three attack vectors and their physical impacts on robotic manipulation.

1

Normal force injection

A passive tag induces a false normal-force response, causing the controller to receive incorrect contact-force feedback.

2

Shear force spoofing

Spatially varying magnetic fields create spoofed shear-force patterns, which can mislead dynamic tactile interpretation.

3

Stealthy deployment

The tag is passive and thin enough to be hidden under common non-ferromagnetic covers, such as stickers or labels.

Q&A

Which tactile sensors are affected by this vulnerability? +

The artifact focuses on commercial off-the-shelf magnetic tactile sensors evaluated in the paper, including PaXini and XELA sensor families. The core issue is related to magnetic-field-based force interpretation.

Can visual monitoring detect the deployed tags? +

Visual detection is challenging when passive tags are hidden under ordinary labels or stickers. The artifact includes demos and figures that illustrate this stealthy deployment model.

Does the artifact require specialized hardware to review? +

Reviewing the scripts, figures, recorded traces, and videos does not require hardware. Fully repeating the physical experiments requires the tactile sensors, robotic platform, and tag materials described in the paper.

How is double-blind review preserved? +

The page intentionally uses anonymous authorship and avoids institutional identifiers. Do not include personal names, lab names, account names, or non-anonymous links in this page during review.
